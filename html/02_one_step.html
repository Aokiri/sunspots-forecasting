One-step-ahead forecasting with a simple RNN¶

In this notebook I train a simple recurrent neural network (RNN) to forecast the sunspot number one month ahead, and compare it with a dense network that sees the same 36-month window. Both are also compared with the two baselines from notebook 01: persistence and the linear auhtoregressive model.

The split, the scaling and the windows come from src/data.py, so every number here can be compared with the ones in notebook 01.

3. Baselines¶

Persistence repeats the last month of the window, and the linear AR model is a linear regression on the 36 months. A network has to beat both to show it learned something that a linear model cannot. I keep their predictions for every part, so the test comparison in section 6 uses exactly the same windows as the networks.

4. Models¶

Both networks receive the same 36-month window and end in a linear layer, since the test period goes above the scaled training range. The simple RNN reads the window one month at a time and forecasts from its last hidden state; the dense network flattens the window and sees all months at once, with no notion of their order.

The hidden size is chosen on validation in section 5. The table below shows the number of trainable parameters of each network for the sizes I compare.

5. Training¶

Both networks are trained with Adam (learning rate $0.001$, batches of $32$ windows) on the MSE of the scaled values, since the error I report is the RMSE. Early stopping watches the validation loss with a patience of $20$ epochs and restores the best weights, and the gradients are clipped to a norm of 1 to keep the recurrent network stable. The settings live in src/training.py, since the next notebooks train the same way.

A single run depends on the random initial weights and on the order of the batches, so every configuration is trained with 10 seeds. The trained weights and their loss histories are saved in models/02_one_step/; setting RETRAIN = True in the setup trains everything again from scratch.

5.1 Hidden size¶

I train each network with $16$, $32$ and $64$ hidden units and choose, for each network separately, the size with the lowest mean validation RMSE over the seeds. The standard error of the mean (sem) shows whether the gap between two sizes is larger than the noise between seeds. Each network gets its own size: if the dense network ends up with more parameters, it only becomes a stronger reference for the RNN.

Findings:

For the simple RNN, $32$ hidden units give the lowest mean validation RMSE ($13.65$). With $64$ units the mean is $13.68$, and both sizes have at sem of $0.02$, so the two are within the noise between seeds and I keep the smaller one. For the dense network the choice is clearer: $64$ units reach $13.76$ against $13.85$ with $32$ units, a gap several times larger than their sems ($0.02$ and $0.03$).

With these sizes the dense network ends up with $2433$ trainable parameters and the RNN with $1153$ (section 4). The dense network matches the linear AR model on validation ($13.76$ in section 3), while the RNN is slightly below both. These numbers only choose the sizes; the comparison that matters is the one on the test period in section 6.

The best epoch also comes earlier as the networks grow, from $76.1$ to $34.3$ epochs on average for the RNN and from $56.4$ to $31.0$ for the dense network: larger networks reach their best validation loss sooner. The chosen sizes are stored in src/models.py as RNN_HIDDEN and DENSE_HIDDEN, so the next notebooks use the same ones.

5.2 Learning curves¶

The curves show whether training converged, where early stopping kept the weights and whether either network start to overfit. Each line is one seed with the hidden size chosen for each network in section 5.1.

Findings:

For the simple RNN, the training and validation losses go down together and stay close for the whole run. The validation loss is still flat or slowly decreasing when early stopping ends the run, so at this size the RNN shows no sign of overfitting.

The dense network behaves differently. Its training loss keeps falling while the validation loss flattens early and become noisier, so the gap between the two grows with the epochs. That is the start of overfitting, and it explains why its best epochs come earlier than the RNN's ($31.0$ against $51.4$ on average in the table of section 5.1). Restoring the best weights keeps the dense network from before the gap opens.

The jumps in the validation curves are expected. The validation set is a single block of consecutive months, so its errors move together from one epoch to the next, and the y-axis is cut to the range where training settles, which makes small changes look large (zoom effect).

6. Test evaluation¶

The test period is used only here, once the sizes are fixed. Each network is evaluated with its 10 seeds, and the baselines with the same windows. Since the test period is more active than validation, every error is also divided by the error of persistence on the same period, so the two periods can be compared.

6.1 Errors¶

In [8]:
# Predictions of the 10 seeds of each network, with shape (seeds, windows, 1) for every part.
network_predictions: dict = {
    name: {
        part: np.stack([predict(runs[(name, chosen_hidden[name], seed)][0], X) for seed in SEEDS])
        for part, (X, _) in windows.items()
    } for name in builders
}

records: list[dict] = []
for part in ("validation", "test"):
    y = windows[part][1]
    for name, preds in predictions.items():
        records.append({"model": name, "part": part, "RMSE": rmse_sunspots(scaler, y, preds[part])})
    for name, preds in network_predictions.items():
        for seed_prediction in preds[part]:
            records.append({"model": name, "part": part, "RMSE": rmse_sunspots(scaler, y, seed_prediction)})
            
errors = pd.DataFrame(records).groupby(["model", "part"], sort=False)["RMSE"]
results = pd.DataFrame({
    "validation RMSE": errors.mean().xs("validation", level="part"),
    "test RMSE": errors.mean().xs("test", level="part"),
    # The baselines are deterministic, so their spread over seeds is NaN.
    "test std over seeds": errors.std().xs("test", level="part"),
})
for part in ("validation", "test"):
    results[f"{part} / persistence"] = results[f"{part} RMSE"] / results.loc["persistence", f"{part} RMSE"]
results.round(2)

Findings:

On the test period every model beats persistence ($20.09$), but the linear AR model has the lowest error ($18.32$). The dense network follows with $18.66$ and the simple RNN with $18.85$, so the order of validation is reversed: the RNN was the best model there ($13.65$) and is the worst of the three on test.

The ratios to persistence remove the effect of the more active test period and show the same change. On validation the RNN is at $0.91$, against $0.92$ for the linear AR model; on test it moves to $0.94$ while the linear AR model stays at $0.91$. The networks also become less consistent: the standard deviation over seeds is $0.48$ for the RNN and $0.44$ for the dense network on test, against $0.05$ and $0.08$ on validation (section 5.1).

One month ahead, neither network learns anything that the linear model does not already capture, and what they learn carries over worse to a period with many more months at high levels than the training data (section 7.1).

Findings:

Over the whole series the one-step forecasts follow the actual values closely, in the test period as well, and they are smoother than the monthly values: the network does not try to predict the month-to-month noise.

The zoom shows where the test error comes from. Around the maximum of 1957 and 1958 the actual values go well above every forecast, so the three models predict the peak too low, the simple RNN lowest of all. Its band is also wider there than on the rising and falling parts of the cycle, which is where the seeds disagree. Away from the maximum the three models almost overlap. The forecasts also reach each sharp change about a month late, a common pattern in one-step forecasts that lean on the last months of the window.

7. Bias by level¶

The errors of section 6 are averages over the whole test period, and the zoom in section 6.2 suggests that most of the error comes from the maximum. Here I check it with the residuals (actual minus forecast, in sunspots), grouped by the level of the actual value. A positive residual means that the forecast was too low. For each network I use the mean forecast of its 10 seeds, the same line as in the figure of section 6.2, so every model has one residual series.

8. Summary¶

The settings that the next notebooks reuse:

  • Training: Adam with a learning rate of $0.001$, batches of 32 windows, MSE loss, gradients clipped to a norm o f 1, and early stopping with a patience of 20 epochs that restores the best weights. Every configuration is trained with 10 seeds (section 5, src/training.py).

  • Sizes: 32 hidden units for the simple RNN and 64 for the dense network, chosen on validation (section 5.1, RNN_HIDDEN and DENSE_HIDDEN in src/models.py).

What the one-step comparison shows:

  • Test errors: both networks beat persistence ($20.09$) but not the linear AR model ($18.32$); the dense network reaches $18.66$ and the simple RNN $18.85$. On validation the RNN was the best model, so the order changes between the two peridos (section 6.1).

  • Where the error comes from: all models forecast the high levels too low, and the networks more than the linear model. Above $154.5$ the mean residual is $26.3$ for the simple RNN, $22.2$ for the dense network and $17.5$ for the linear AR model. Only 25 training targets are at those levels, against 65 months in the test period (section 7). The recursive forecasts of the next notebook feed each forecast back as input, so this bias can add up over the 6 months.

  • Open point: persistence has the smallest bias at high levels ($10.5$), because it starts from the last month. A network that predicts the change from the last month of the window, instead of the level, would start from the same point. I did not test it here.
